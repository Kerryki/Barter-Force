When you look at a mortgage offer, two time periods appear side by side: the term and the amortization. Many people assume they are the same. They are not, and understanding the difference is one of the simplest ways to feel more in control of your mortgage.

## The amortization: how long to repay everything

The amortization is the total length of time it would take to repay the whole loan, if every payment were made on schedule. In Canada, a common amortization is 25 years, though shorter and, in some situations, longer periods exist. The rules depend on your down payment and the lender.

Think of it as the full journey. A longer amortization means smaller regular payments, but you pay interest for more years, so the total interest cost is higher. A shorter amortization raises each payment but reduces the total interest you pay over the life of the loan.

## The term: how long your deal lasts

The term is much shorter. It is the length of the contract you sign with a lender for a specific interest rate and set of conditions. Terms are commonly between one and ten years, with five years being the most familiar.

When your term ends, you do not owe the balance in full. Your mortgage comes up for renewal. At that point you can renew with the same lender, switch to another lender, or pay the balance down if you can. You then choose a new term at whatever rates are available at that time.

## An everyday comparison

Imagine planning a 25-year road trip across the country. The amortization is the full distance. The term is one stretch of five years, booked with a particular driver at a price agreed in advance. When that stretch ends, you choose the next driver and the next price, but the destination is still the same.

## Why the difference matters

**Your rate can change at renewal.** If rates are lower when your term ends, your payments may fall. If they are higher, your payments may rise. That is why it helps to plan for a range of rates rather than assuming today's rate will last for 25 years.

**You can choose to change the amortization.** At renewal, some borrowers lengthen the amortization to lower payments, and others shorten it to pay less interest overall. Each choice has a cost, and it is worth seeing the numbers before deciding.

**Penalties usually relate to the term.** If you want to leave your contract before the term ends, for example to sell or refinance, the lender may charge a penalty. The size of the penalty depends on your type of rate and your lender's rules, so ask for a clear explanation before you sign.

## Questions to ask before you sign

- What is the term, and when does it end?
- What is the amortization, and how much interest will I pay over that period at today's rate?
- What happens to my payment if the rate is different at renewal?
- What would it cost me to leave early?
- Can I make extra payments, and how much without a charge?

## Common questions

**Can I have a 25-year term?** In practice, no. Terms are far shorter than amortizations, because lenders prefer to reset the rate periodically. The long period is the amortization, which only describes the repayment schedule.

**Does a shorter amortization always make sense?** Not necessarily. It saves interest, but it raises your payment. If the higher payment would leave you with no room for emergencies, a longer amortization with occasional extra payments may be more comfortable.

**What happens to my balance at the end of the term?** It carries forward into the next term. Nothing is lost, and the amount you still owe is simply the starting point for your renewal.

If your renewal letter ever arrives and the figures look unfamiliar, ask your lender to show the remaining balance and the remaining amortization. Those two numbers tell you exactly where you stand.

## A simple way to remember it

The term is the contract. The amortization is the plan to be debt-free. Most people will go through several terms before the amortization is finished, and each renewal is a chance to compare offers instead of accepting the first one you are sent.

If you are not sure what your own offer says, bring it to a conversation. Understanding every number before you agree to it is the point.

## Key takeaways

- The amortization is the total repayment period. The term is the length of one contract.
- A longer amortization lowers payments but increases total interest.
- Your rate is reset at each renewal, so plan for payments that could be higher or lower.
- Always ask what leaving the contract early would cost, and keep the offer in front of you while you ask.
- Write down your term end date today, so it never arrives as a surprise.
