Canada offers several registered accounts that can help your money grow with tax advantages. The names sound similar, and the rules differ. Here is a simple way to understand the three most common ones for people starting out.

## First, an important idea

A TFSA, an RRSP or an FHSA is a type of account, not an investment. Inside each account you can hold different things, such as a savings deposit, a guaranteed investment certificate (GIC), or investment funds. The account decides how the money is taxed. What you hold inside decides how it grows and how much risk you take.

## The TFSA: Tax-Free Savings Account

- You contribute money that has already been taxed. There is no tax deduction for contributions.
- Growth inside the account is not taxed.
- Withdrawals are not taxed.
- If you withdraw money, the amount is added back to your contribution room, generally at the start of the following calendar year.
- Each year you receive new contribution room, and unused room carries forward.

The TFSA is flexible. It suits emergency funds, medium-term goals and long-term savings. Because withdrawals are not taxed and the room returns, it is often a good first account.

## The RRSP: Registered Retirement Savings Plan

- Contributions are generally tax-deductible, which can reduce your income tax for the year.
- Growth inside the account is not taxed while it stays there.
- Withdrawals are added to your income and taxed at that time.
- Your contribution room depends on your earned income in previous years, up to a yearly maximum, and unused room carries forward.

The idea is to receive the tax break now, while your income may be higher, and pay tax later, when your income may be lower in retirement. It can be particularly helpful when your income is higher. The RRSP also has special withdrawal programs for buying a first home or going back to school, with conditions to follow.

## The FHSA: First Home Savings Account

The FHSA is designed for people saving for a first home. It combines features of the other two accounts:

- Contributions are generally tax-deductible, like an RRSP.
- Withdrawals for a qualifying first home purchase are not taxed, like a TFSA.
- You must meet eligibility rules, including that you are a first-time buyer under the program's definition, and there are annual and lifetime limits.
- The account must be used or closed within a set period.

If you plan to buy a first home in Canada, it may be worth discussing the FHSA early, since the benefits build with time.

## How to choose

There is no universal answer, but these questions help:

- **Is the money for a home?** Look at the FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan.
- **Might you need the money soon, or want flexibility?** The TFSA is usually the stronger fit.
- **Is the money for retirement and your income is fairly high?** The RRSP's deduction can be valuable.
- **Are you unsure?** A TFSA is often a reasonable place to start while you learn.

Many people use more than one account, and the order that makes sense depends on income, goals and timing.

## Watch for these mistakes

- **Over-contributing.** Each account has limits, and going over can lead to penalties. Check your room with the Canada Revenue Agency before contributing.
- **Leaving cash idle.** Money in a registered account can still earn little if it is not invested in line with your goals.
- **Ignoring fees.** Fees reduce growth over time, so understand what you pay for each product.
- **Confusing the account with the investment.** A TFSA full of low-return savings is still a TFSA, but it may not meet a long-term goal.

## Limits change

Contribution limits and rules are updated over time. Always confirm the current figures with the Canada Revenue Agency or a qualified professional before you act.

## A simple example

Imagine someone who earns a steady salary and wants three things: an emergency fund, a first home in a few years and, eventually, retirement. They might keep the emergency fund in a TFSA for easy access, use an FHSA for the home down payment, and add to an RRSP once the other two are underway. Another person with a different income or timeline could reasonably choose a different order. The point is to match each account to a goal.

## Common questions

**Can I have all three accounts?** Yes, if you are eligible. Each has its own rules and limits.

**Which is better for retirement, the TFSA or the RRSP?** Both can be used. The RRSP's deduction can be more valuable when your income is higher, while the TFSA offers flexibility and tax-free withdrawals. Many people use both over time.

**What happens to an RRSP or FHSA if I do not use it as planned?** The rules differ for each, including timing and tax treatment on withdrawal, so check the details before moving money.

## Key takeaways

- The account decides the tax treatment. What you hold inside decides growth and risk.
- TFSA: tax-free growth and withdrawals, with flexible access.
- RRSP: deduction now, tax later, aimed at retirement.
- FHSA: deduction and tax-free withdrawal for a qualifying first home.
- Confirm your contribution room and the current limits before you contribute.
