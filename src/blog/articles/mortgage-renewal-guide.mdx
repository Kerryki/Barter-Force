Every mortgage term ends. When yours does, your lender will usually send a renewal letter with a new rate. It is easy to sign it and move on. But accepting the first offer can cost more than you expect, so it pays to treat renewal as a decision rather than a formality.

## What actually happens at renewal

At the end of your term you are free to choose. You can renew with your current lender, move your mortgage to a different lender, or, if you have the means, repay some or all of the balance. Many lenders contact you a few months before the end date, and some send the offer close to the deadline.

If you do nothing, many mortgages renew automatically, often for a short term at a rate that may not be competitive. Check your contract to see what applies to you.

## Start early

Aim to begin reviewing your options about four to six months before your renewal date. Many lenders allow you to lock in a rate in advance, and some let you switch or renew early, though early renewal can involve a penalty. Starting early gives you time to compare calmly.

## Compare more than the rate

A lower rate is attractive, but it is only part of the picture. Compare:

- **The rate and the term.** A shorter or longer term changes how soon you will face renewal again.
- **Fixed or variable.** Your situation may have changed since you first chose.
- **Prepayment privileges.** How much extra can you pay each year without a charge?
- **Penalty rules.** How would leaving early be calculated?
- **Amortization.** You may be able to keep, shorten or, in some cases, lengthen it. Each choice affects the total interest.
- **Fees.** Switching to another lender can involve legal or discharge fees, though some lenders cover those costs.

## Switching lenders

In many cases you can move to a new lender at renewal without paying a penalty, because you are at the end of your term. A new lender will usually check your income and credit, and may ask for an appraisal. Ask who pays for what before you decide. If a competing lender offers a better rate, your current lender may also be willing to improve its own offer, so it can be worth asking.

## What if my situation has changed?

Renewal is a natural moment to ask bigger questions:

- Has my income gone up or down?
- Do I want to pay down the mortgage faster?
- Do I need to borrow for a renovation or to consolidate other debts?
- Do I plan to move in the next few years?

Your answers may point toward a different term, a different rate type or a different lender than last time.

## Check your credit before you apply

If you are considering a new lender, review your credit report beforehand. Errors happen, and it is easier to fix them before an application than during one.

## A short renewal checklist

1. Find your renewal date and read your current contract.
2. Request your renewal offer and note the deadline.
3. Gather recent income documents.
4. Compare at least two other offers.
5. Ask about prepayment privileges and penalties for each.
6. Decide, then confirm everything in writing.
7. Set a reminder for the next renewal.

## The key idea

Your lender's renewal offer is a starting point, not a final price. A little time spent comparing at renewal can make a meaningful difference over the next term, and it ensures that the mortgage still matches your life.

## Common questions

**Will my credit be checked if I stay with my lender?** Often not, but practices vary. If you switch, expect a full application.

**What if rates have gone up since my last term?** Your payment may rise. Consider whether a longer amortization, a different term or extra payments could help, and weigh the cost of each.

**Is it too late if my renewal is in a few weeks?** No, but you have less leverage and less time. Request your offer right away and compare it with at least one alternative before signing.

## A worked comparison

Suppose you have two offers. The first has a slightly lower rate but a strict penalty and limited prepayment options. The second has a slightly higher rate, allows larger yearly extra payments and uses a simpler penalty. If you expect a bonus next year that you want to put toward the mortgage, or you may move in three years, the second offer could cost you less in practice despite the higher rate. Placing the offers side by side, with your own plans in mind, is how you find out.

## Key takeaways

- Do not sign the first renewal letter without comparing it with other offers.
- Begin four to six months before the end of your term.
- Look at penalties, prepayment privileges and amortization, not only the rate.
- Review your credit and your goals before you decide.
