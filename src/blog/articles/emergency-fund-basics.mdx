Before investing, before a big purchase, and often before an aggressive plan to pay off debt, most financial guidance begins with the same step: build a cushion. An emergency fund is money set aside for the unexpected, so that a surprise bill does not become a financial crisis.

## What counts as an emergency

An emergency is something unexpected and necessary: a sudden loss of income, an urgent car or home repair, a medical or dental cost not covered by your plan, or travel for a family crisis. A sale, a holiday or a planned renovation is not an emergency. Those belong in separate savings goals.

Keeping the definition clear helps protect the fund from being spent on everyday wants.

## How much is enough?

A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. Essential means the costs you must pay to keep going: housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments and similar items.

Your own number may be higher or lower:

- **A larger fund** may suit self-employed people, those with variable income, single-income households or people with dependants.
- **A smaller starter fund** is still valuable. Even one month of essentials can prevent a small problem from turning to high-interest debt.

To estimate your target, add up your monthly essentials and multiply by the number of months you want to cover.

## Where to keep it

Your emergency fund has three jobs: to be safe, to be available and to be separate. Look for something that:

- Keeps your money stable, so a market drop does not shrink it when you need it.
- Allows quick access without penalties.
- Is easy to separate from your everyday account, so you do not spend it by accident.

A high-interest savings account is a common choice. A TFSA can also work well as the account, since withdrawals are not taxed and the room is returned later, but the money inside should be kept in a stable, easily accessible form such as savings rather than volatile investments.

## How to build it when money is tight

Starting small is better than waiting. Here are practical ways:

1. **Set a first target** such as a small amount that covers a typical car repair, then raise it.
2. **Automate.** Set up a transfer for the day after you are paid, even if the amount is modest. What you do not see, you do not spend.
3. **Use windfalls.** A tax refund, a bonus or a gift can give your fund a head start.
4. **Review recurring costs.** An unused subscription or a better insurance quote can free some money.
5. **Track progress.** Seeing the balance grow is motivating.

## Emergency fund or debt first?

It depends. If you carry high-interest debt, paying it down matters. But with no cushion at all, an unexpected expense may push you right back onto your card. Many people build a small starter fund first, then pay down debt with a firm plan, and then build the fund to its full target.

## What to do after you use it

Using your fund is exactly what it is for, not a failure. After an emergency, rebuild it as soon as you can, using the same automatic transfer you began with.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Keeping it in the same account as daily spending.
- Investing it in assets that can drop sharply.
- Treating non-emergencies as emergencies.
- Giving up after one withdrawal.

## Common questions

**Should my emergency fund be in cash at home?** Generally not. Money kept at home earns nothing and can be lost or stolen. A savings account at a deposit-insured institution is usually safer while remaining easy to reach.

**What if I have a credit card as a backup?** A card can bridge a short gap, but borrowing at card rates turns the emergency into a debt. A fund lets you avoid that cost.

**Do couples need one fund or two?** Either can work. What matters is that both people know where it is, what it is for and when it can be used.

**Is a line of credit a substitute?** A line of credit is a loan, so it must be repaid with interest and its availability can change. It can complement a fund, but it does not replace one.

If you are unsure where to begin, pick a figure that feels almost too small, and start this month. Habits matter more than the first amount, and you can always raise the target once the routine is in place.

## Key takeaways

- Aim for three to six months of essential expenses, and start with whatever you can.
- Keep it safe, accessible and separate.
- Automate contributions and rebuild after use.
- It is the base that makes every other goal more achievable.
