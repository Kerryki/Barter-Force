If your credit history is thin, damaged or simply not what you would like, you are far from alone. Newcomers, young adults, and people recovering from illness, job loss or a separation all face this. The good news is that credit responds to steady behaviour over time. The less welcome news is that there are no shortcuts, so a realistic plan matters.

## Step 1: Look at where you stand

Start by getting your credit report from both Equifax and TransUnion. Read each one slowly. Note:

- Accounts that are open and in good standing.
- Late payments, collections or accounts in default.
- Balances compared with limits.
- Anything you do not recognise.

You cannot make a plan without knowing what you are working with, and this step costs nothing.

## Step 2: Correct what is wrong

Mistakes occur: a wrong balance, an account that is not yours, or a debt that was already paid. If you find an error, contact the credit bureau and the creditor in writing to dispute it, and keep copies of what you send. Correcting a genuine error can improve your report without changing any of your habits.

## Step 3: Cover the essentials first

Make sure rent, utilities, insurance and food are secure. Credit repair should not come at the cost of your basic stability. Then list your debts with their balances, interest rates and minimum payments, so you can see the whole picture on one page.

## Step 4: Never miss a payment from today forward

Payment history is the heaviest influence on your score. Set up automatic payments for at least the minimum on every account, and add reminders for the due dates. If you cannot make a payment, contact the creditor before it is late. Many are willing to discuss options, and it is better to talk early.

## Step 5: Bring balances down

If you carry card balances close to your limits, reducing them can help your score and your finances at the same time. Two common methods are:

- **Highest interest first.** You pay the minimum on everything, and put extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate. This usually saves the most money.
- **Smallest balance first.** You clear the smallest debt first for a quick win, then move on. This can help motivation.

Either works. The best method is the one you will keep doing.

## Step 6: Build positive history

If you have little or no credit, you need accounts that report your good behaviour to the bureaus. Options include:

- **A secured credit card**, where you provide a deposit that usually sets your limit. Use it for a small recurring cost and pay the statement in full.
- **A small instalment loan** from a lender that reports to the bureaus.
- **A credit-builder product** offered by some institutions.

Ask any provider whether it reports to both bureaus, and read the fees carefully. Borrow only what you can comfortably repay.

## Step 7: Be patient with new applications

Each application can create an inquiry. Applying for many products in a short period can pull your score down temporarily. Space applications out and apply only when you have a reason.

## Step 8: Keep going, and keep checking

Review your reports every few months to see progress and catch problems. Negative items generally fade in importance as they age, and eventually drop off the report according to the rules that apply to them. Positive habits add up over time.

## How long does it take?

It depends on your starting point. Small improvements may appear within a few months of consistent on-time payments and lower balances. Recovering from serious problems such as collections or a consumer proposal can take years. Be wary of anyone who promises a specific result in a fixed time.

## Warning signs of a poor service

- A promise to remove accurate negative information.
- A large upfront fee before any work is done.
- Pressure to act immediately.
- Advice to create a new identity or to dispute everything.

If you are struggling with debt, a non-profit credit counselling service can help you look at options without selling you anything.

## Common questions

**Do I need a credit card to build credit?** Not strictly, but cards are the most common way to create a reporting history. An instalment loan can serve the same purpose.

**I am new to Canada. Does my foreign history count?** Generally, Canadian bureaus do not carry over your history from another country, so you usually start building from the beginning. Some institutions have programs for newcomers, so ask what is available.

**Should I close accounts with negative history?** Not necessarily. Closing an account does not remove past information and may shorten your history. Ask for guidance before closing anything.

## Key takeaways

- Start with your reports and fix errors.
- Pay on time, always, from now on.
- Lower balances and add positive history carefully.
- Expect progress over months and years, not days.

You can also rebuild with support. If you would like to go through your report together, a calm conversation is a good place to begin.
