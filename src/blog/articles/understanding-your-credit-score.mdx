Your credit score is one of those numbers that quietly affects your life, from approving a mortgage to renting an apartment or getting a better rate on a car loan. Yet few of us were ever taught how it works. This guide explains it in plain terms.

## What a credit score is

A credit score is a number that summarises how you have handled credit in the past. Lenders use it as one input when deciding whether to lend to you and at what rate. In Canada, scores generally range from 300 to 900, and a higher number indicates a stronger credit history.

Two main credit bureaus, Equifax and TransUnion, collect information about your credit accounts and produce reports and scores. Because they may hold slightly different information, your scores from each can differ.

## Score versus report

These two things are related but different:

- **Your credit report** is the detailed record: your accounts, balances, payment history, inquiries and any collections.
- **Your credit score** is a calculation based on that report.

If you want to understand your score, start with the report. It shows what is behind the number, and it is where you can find mistakes.

## What influences your score

Each bureau has its own formula, but the main factors are consistent.

**Payment history.** Paying on time is the strongest influence. Late or missed payments can lower your score and remain on your report for years.

**How much of your limit you use.** This is often called credit utilization. If you have a card with a $5,000 limit and carry a $4,500 balance, you are using most of what is available, which can weigh on your score. Many people aim to keep balances well below their limits.

**Length of credit history.** A longer history gives lenders more to look at. Closing your oldest account can shorten your history.

**New credit and inquiries.** Applying for several new accounts in a short time can lower your score temporarily. When a lender checks your credit for an application, it is recorded as an inquiry. Checking your own credit is different and does not affect your score.

**Mix of credit.** Having different types of credit, such as a credit card and an instalment loan, can help, but it is not a reason to borrow when you do not need to.

## Common myths

**"Checking my own score hurts it."** Looking at your own report or score is not what lenders do when you apply, and it does not reduce your score.

**"I should carry a balance to build credit."** You do not need to pay interest to build credit. Using a card and paying the statement balance in full on time is usually a better habit.

**"Closing cards always helps."** Closing an account can reduce your available credit and shorten your history, which may lower your score.

**"Income is part of my score."** Your income is not part of your credit report, though lenders consider it separately when you apply.

## How to check your credit

You are entitled to request your credit report from each bureau, and doing so is free by mail or through the processes they provide. Review it for accounts you do not recognise, wrong balances and outdated information. If something is incorrect, you can dispute it with the bureau and the creditor.

## Habits that support a healthier score

- Pay at least the minimum on time, and the full balance when you can.
- Keep card balances comfortably below your limits.
- Avoid opening many accounts at once.
- Keep older accounts open if they have no fees and you manage them well.
- Review your reports regularly.

## Be careful of quick-fix promises

No legitimate service can promise to erase accurate negative information from your report or to raise your score overnight. Be cautious about upfront fees and guarantees. Improvement usually comes from steady habits over months and years.

## Common questions

**What is a good score?** Ranges differ between lenders, but higher is better, and the number matters less than the whole picture. A few points will rarely decide an application, but a pattern of late payments can.

**Why do my two scores differ?** Equifax and TransUnion may hold different information and use different formulas, so a gap between them is normal.

**How often does my score change?** It can change whenever information on your report is updated, which is typically monthly. Do not worry about small movements.

**Will paying off a debt raise my score right away?** It can help, particularly if it lowers your balances relative to your limits, but the effect varies. Consistent habits matter more than any single action.

## Key takeaways

- Your score summarises your report, and your report is the place to start.
- On-time payments and moderate balances have the biggest influence.
- Checking your own credit does not hurt your score.
- A history is a record, not a verdict. It can be improved.

If you are planning a major purchase such as a home or a vehicle, reviewing your credit a few months ahead gives you time to act on what you find.
