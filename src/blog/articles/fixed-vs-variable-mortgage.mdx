"Should I take a fixed or a variable rate?" is among the most common mortgage questions, and there is no answer that fits everyone. Anyone who tells you one is always better is guessing about the future. A more useful approach is to understand how each works and then ask which suits your situation.

## How a fixed rate works

With a fixed rate, the interest rate stays the same for the whole term. If your term is five years, your rate does not change for five years, even if market rates move up or down.

The main benefit is predictability. You know what your payments will be and can plan your budget around them. The trade-off is that if market rates fall, you stay at your higher rate until the term ends, unless you pay a penalty to leave.

## How a variable rate works

A variable rate moves with the lender's prime rate, which in turn follows broader market conditions. Lenders handle this in different ways. Some change your payment when the rate changes. Others keep the payment the same and change how much goes to interest and how much to principal. In that second case, a large rate increase can mean your payment no longer covers the interest, which is something to ask about directly.

The potential benefit is that you may pay less if rates fall, and variable mortgages often have a simpler penalty for leaving early. The trade-off is uncertainty: your costs can rise as well as fall.

## The comparison people forget: the penalty

Breaking a mortgage before the end of the term can be expensive, and the cost differs by rate type.

- Variable rate penalties are commonly based on a few months of interest.
- Fixed rate penalties are commonly the greater of a few months of interest or an amount based on the difference between your rate and the lender's current rate, often called the interest rate differential.

The exact method varies by lender, and some are far more generous than others. If there is a reasonable chance you will sell, move or refinance within your term, the penalty can matter more than the rate itself.

## Questions to ask yourself

**How much would a payment increase hurt?** If a higher payment would cause real stress, a fixed rate may let you sleep better, even if it costs a little more on paper. If you have a comfortable cushion, you may be able to accept variable-rate movement.

**How long do I expect to stay?** If you may sell or refinance soon, ask what your penalty would be under each option.

**What does my income look like?** Stable income makes it easier to absorb change. Income that varies from month to month may call for more predictability.

**What else do I have going on?** A new baby, a career change or a renovation can all affect how much risk feels comfortable.

## Comparing offers fairly

A lower rate does not always mean a better mortgage. When comparing, look at:

- The rate and the type (fixed or variable).
- The term and the amortization.
- The penalty rules, including how they are calculated.
- Prepayment options: how much extra you can pay each year without a charge.
- Whether the mortgage is portable if you move.

Putting the options side by side, including the cost of leaving early, usually makes the right choice clearer than the headline rate alone.

## No one can predict rates

Economists with far more data than any of us do not reliably predict where rates will go. A better question than "which will win?" is "which can I live with if I am wrong?" Choosing the option that works in both a good and a bad scenario is often more valuable than trying to beat the market.

## Common questions

**Can I switch from variable to fixed during my term?** Many lenders allow it, often without a penalty, but the fixed rate offered at that moment may be higher than the one you could have had earlier. Ask what the conversion rules are before you choose a variable rate.

**Is a variable rate riskier?** It carries more uncertainty, but "riskier" depends on your budget. A household with a comfortable margin can handle movement better than one living close to the limit.

**Does the lowest rate on a comparison site always win?** No. A slightly higher rate with flexible prepayment options and a fair penalty can cost less overall if your plans change.

## Key takeaways

- Fixed means a predictable payment. Variable means a rate that moves with the market.
- The penalty for leaving early often matters more than a small difference in rate.
- Choose the option you can live with if you turn out to be wrong about rates.
- Compare prepayment options, portability and conversion rules, not only the headline number.
